A distributed simulation stores $$n$$ objects of a $$d$$-dimensional space on $$p$$ ranks. A spatial decomposition assigns each object to a rank, with three goals:
- **balance**: every rank holds $$n/p$$ objects. This is a [load balancing][lb] problem;
- **locality**: the objects of a rank are close to each other, so interactions between close objects need no communication;
- **low duplication**: an object with a volume that crosses the boundary between two regions is stored on both ranks. These copies cost memory and communication.

[lb]: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Load_balancing_(computing)

## Slicing layouts

The simplest decompositions, called **slicing**, cut the space with planes orthogonal to the axes. The figure shows four slicing layouts in 2D:

<img width="18%" height="18%" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-nug.svg">
<img width="18%" height="18%" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-orb.svg">
<img width="18%" height="18%" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-str.svg">

From left to right:
1. **single axis**: cuts the space along one axis only;
2. **non-uniform grid** (NUG): cuts each axis $$k$$ into $$S_k$$ intervals with equal object counts. The cuts form a grid of $$S_1 \times S_2$$ slices;
3. **orthogonal recursive bisection** (ORB): cuts every region into two halves with equal object counts, along the axes in turn, $$\log_2 p$$ times;
4. **sort-tile-recursive** (STR), a bulk-loading method of [R-trees][rtree]: cuts the space along the first axis into $$S_1$$ regions with equal object counts, then each region along the second axis into $$S_2$$ slices. Each region has its own cuts.

[rtree]: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-tree

The single-axis layout gives thin slices for large $$p$$. In a unit cube with uniform data, a thin slice shares two faces of area 1 with its neighbors. A cubic slice of the same volume shares $$2d$$ faces of area $$p^{-(d-1)/d}$$. The boundary of the thin slice is thus $$p^{(d-1)/d}/d$$ times larger: 4 times for $$d=2$$ and $$p=64$$, and 21 times for $$d=3$$ and $$p=512$$. The duplicated objects and the communication with neighbor ranks grow by the same factor.

The NUG slices are compact, but not balanced. All slices share the cuts of each axis, so their object counts can differ widely: for points on the diagonal of a square, only the $$S$$ diagonal slices of an $$S \times S$$ grid hold objects. ORB and STR give compact slices with equal object counts.

The cost of a layout is the cost of its sorts, with $$m \log_2 m$$ steps to sort $$m$$ objects:

| Layout | Sort steps | Properties |
|:-:|:--|:--|
| <img width="70" height="70" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-single-axis.svg"><br/>**Single Axis** | $$n \log_2 n$$ | One sort of all objects along one axis, whatever $$d$$. |
| <img width="70" height="70" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-nug.svg"><br/>**Non-Uniform Grid** | $$d \, n \log_2 n$$ | One sort of all objects along each axis. The numbers of intervals satisfy $$\prod_{k=1}^d S_k = p$$. |
| <img width="70" height="70" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-orb.svg"><br/>**Orthogonal Recursive Bisection** | $$\begin{array}{l} n \log_2 n \\ + \, n \log_2 \frac{n}{2} \\ + \, n \log_2 \frac{n}{4} \\ + \cdots \\ + \, n \log_2 \frac{2n}{p} \\ = \sum_{i=1}^{\log_2 p} n \log_2 \frac{n}{2^{i-1}} \end{array}$$ | $$p$$ is a power of 2. Level $$i$$ sorts each of its $$2^{i-1}$$ regions, independently, and cuts it at its median. |
| <img width="70" height="70" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-str.svg"><br/>**Sort Tile Recursive** | $$\begin{array}{l} n \log_2 n \\ + \, n \log_2 \frac{n}{S_1} \\ + \, n \log_2 \frac{n}{S_1 S_2} \\ + \cdots \\ + \, n \log_2 \frac{n}{S_1 S_2 \cdots S_{d-1}} \\ = \sum_{i=1}^d n \log_2 \frac{n}{\prod_{k=0}^{i-1} S_k} \end{array}$$ | $$S_0 = 1$$ and $$\prod_{k=1}^d S_k = p$$. Dimension $$i$$ sorts each of the $$\prod_{k=0}^{i-1} S_k$$ regions of the previous cuts, independently. |

For $$n = 2^{20}$$ objects in 2D on $$p = 64$$ ranks, with $$S_1 = S_2 = 8$$, the costs are $$20n$$ (single axis), $$40n$$ (NUG), $$105n$$ (ORB) and $$37n$$ (STR). STR costs less than NUG, since each dimension sorts smaller sets than the one before. On distributed memory, each level of sorts is also a round of communication: ORB takes $$\log_2 p$$ rounds, and STR takes $$d$$ rounds, whatever $$p$$. STR thus combines compact and balanced slices with few rounds.

## Distributed slicing with Sort-Balance-Split

On distributed memory, the objects of a region are spread over many ranks, so finding its exact cuts takes communication. Two common methods approximate the cuts:
- **histogram**: each rank counts its objects in spatial bins, a reduction sums the counts of all ranks, and the cuts fall on bin edges. This takes one pass over the objects and one reduction per dimension, but a cut is only as precise as a bin;
- **sampling**: one rank gathers a sample of the objects, slices it serially, and broadcasts the cuts. The sample must fit in the memory of one rank, and the balance is only as precise as the sample.

[Sort-Balance-Split][slicing-ref] (SBS) computes the exact cuts with a distributed sort. It processes one dimension per round. In round $$k$$, the ranks run three steps:
- **sort**: a [distributed sample sort]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2014-06-21-distributed-sort %}) orders the objects along axis $$k$$;
- **balance**: the ranks exchange their object counts. From the counts, each rank computes the global index of its objects, and sends each object to the rank that owns its index after the balance. The sort order is kept, and the object counts of any two ranks differ by at most one:

{: .picture}
 <img width="82%" height="82%" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/balance.svg">

- **split**: `MPI_Comm_split` divides the ranks into $$S_k$$ groups of consecutive ranks, called subnetworks. Each subnetwork holds one region, and runs the next round on it, independently of the others:

{: .picture}
 <img width="79%" height="79%" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/split.svg">

After $$d$$ rounds, each subnetwork is a single rank, which holds one slice. The number of communication steps is fixed, whatever $$n$$, $$p$$ and the data distribution. SBS trades communication for balance. The histogram and sampling methods move each object once, to its final rank. SBS moves the objects in every round, in the sort and in the balance.
