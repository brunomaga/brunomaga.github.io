Consider $$n$$ objects placed in a $$d$$-dimensional space and stored across $$p$$ processes of a distributed system, called **ranks** in [MPI][mpi]. This setup is common in large-scale scientific applications, and it raises three questions, which are the three goals of a spatial decomposition:
- **balance**: how to divide the space so that every rank holds $$n/p$$ objects? This is a [load balancing][lb] problem;
- **locality**: how to keep the objects of each rank close to each other, so that interactions between close objects need no communication?
- **low duplication**: how to keep the overlap between regions small? An object with a volume that crosses the boundary between two regions is stored on both ranks, and these copies cost memory and communication.

[mpi]: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Message_Passing_Interface
[lb]: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Load_balancing_(computing)

## Slicing layouts

The simplest decompositions cut the space with planes orthogonal to the axes, and are called **slicing**. Even in 2D, several slicing layouts are possible:

<img width="20.1%" height="20.1%" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-nug.svg">
<img width="20%" height="20%" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-orb.svg">

From left to right:
1. **single axis** cuts the space along one axis only;
2. **non-uniform grid** (NUG) cuts each axis $$k$$ into $$S_k$$ intervals with equal object counts, which form a grid of $$S_1 \times S_2$$ slices;
3. **orthogonal recursive bisection** (ORB) cuts every region into two halves with equal object counts, along the axes in turn, $$\log_2 p$$ times;
4. **sort-tile-recursive** (STR), a bulk-loading method of [R-trees][rtree], cuts the space along the first axis into $$S_1$$ regions with equal object counts, and then each region, with cuts of its own, along the second axis into $$S_2$$ slices.

[rtree]: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-tree

For large $$p$$, the single-axis layout gives thin slices with long boundaries. In a unit cube with uniform data, a thin slice shares two faces of area 1 with its neighbors, while a cubic slice of the same volume shares $$2d$$ faces of area $$p^{-(d-1)/d}$$. The boundary of the thin slice is therefore $$p^{(d-1)/d}/d$$ times larger: 4 times for $$d=2$$ and $$p=64$$, and 21 times for $$d=3$$ and $$p=512$$. The duplicated objects and the communication with neighbor ranks grow by the same factor.

The NUG slices are compact, but not always balanced, because all slices share the cuts of each axis: for points on the diagonal of a square and $$S_1 = S_2$$, only the $$S_1$$ slices on the diagonal hold objects. ORB and STR cut each region on its own, so their slices are both compact and balanced. ORB cuts every region in two at each level of a tree, while STR cuts a region into all its slices along an axis at once, which allows for more parallelism.

What does each layout cost? A cut with equal object counts on both sides is a position in the sorted order of its region (the median, for a bisection), so the cost of a layout is the cost of its sorts, with $$m \log_2 m$$ steps to sort $$m$$ objects:

| Layout | Sort steps | Properties |
|:-:|:--|:--|
| <img width="70" height="70" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-single-axis.svg"><br/>**Single Axis** | $$n \log_2 n$$ | One sort of all objects along one axis, whatever $$d$$. |
| <img width="70" height="70" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-nug.svg"><br/>**Non-Uniform Grid** | $$d \, n \log_2 n$$ | One sort of all objects along each axis. The numbers of intervals satisfy $$\prod_{k=1}^d S_k = p$$. |
| <img width="70" height="70" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-str.svg"><br/>**Sort Tile Recursive** | $$\begin{array}{l} n \log_2 n \\ + \, n \log_2 \frac{n}{S_1} \\ + \, n \log_2 \frac{n}{S_1 S_2} \\ + \cdots \\ + \, n \log_2 \frac{n}{S_1 S_2 \cdots S_{d-1}} \\ = \sum_{i=1}^d n \log_2 \frac{n}{\prod_{k=0}^{i-1} S_k} \end{array}$$ | $$S_0 = 1$$ and $$\prod_{k=1}^d S_k = p$$. Dimension $$i$$ sorts each of the $$\prod_{k=0}^{i-1} S_k$$ regions of the previous cuts, independently. |
| <img width="70" height="70" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/mini-orb.svg"><br/>**Orthogonal Recursive Bisection** | $$\begin{array}{l} n \log_2 n \\ + \, n \log_2 \frac{n}{2} \\ + \, n \log_2 \frac{n}{4} \\ + \cdots \\ + \, n \log_2 \frac{2n}{p} \\ = \sum_{i=1}^{\log_2 p} n \log_2 \frac{n}{2^{i-1}} \end{array}$$ | $$p$$ is a power of 2. Level $$i$$ sorts each of its $$2^{i-1}$$ regions, independently, and cuts it at its median. |

For $$n = 2^{20}$$ objects in 2D on $$p = 64$$ ranks, with $$S_1 = S_2 = 8$$, the costs are $$20n$$ for the single axis, $$40n$$ for the NUG, $$105n$$ for ORB and $$37n$$ for STR. STR costs less than the NUG because its second sort runs on 8 regions of $$2^{17}$$ objects, instead of on all $$2^{20}$$ objects. On distributed memory, each level of sorts is also a round of communication: ORB takes $$\log_2 p$$ rounds, which grow with the number of ranks, and STR only $$d$$. STR therefore offers the best trade-off, with compact and balanced slices, cheaper sorts and few rounds. Its distributed implementation, however, is not trivial.

## Distributed slicing with Sort-Balance-Split

What makes STR hard on distributed memory? The objects of a region are spread over many ranks, so finding its exact cuts takes communication. Two common methods approximate the cuts:
- **histogram**: each rank counts its objects in spatial bins, a reduction sums the counts of all ranks, and the cuts fall on bin edges. This takes one pass over the objects and one reduction per dimension, but a cut is only as precise as a bin;
- **sampling**: one rank gathers a sample of the objects, slices it serially, and broadcasts the cuts. The sample must fit in the memory of one rank, and the balance is only as precise as the sample.

[Sort-Balance-Split][slicing-ref] (SBS), a method published by the author, computes the exact cuts instead, with a distributed sort. It processes one dimension per round, and in round $$k$$ the ranks run the three steps that give the method its name:
- **sort**: a [distributed sample sort]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2014-06-21-distributed-sort %}) orders the objects along axis $$k$$, but leaves the ranks with different numbers of objects;
- **balance**: the ranks exchange their object counts, and a [prefix sum][prefix] of the counts gives each rank the global index of its objects. Each rank then sends every object to the rank that owns its index after the balance, so the sort order is kept, and the object counts of any two ranks differ by at most one. In the figure, rank 0 sends its last two objects to rank 1, and rank 1 its last two to rank 2:

Load balancing of 19 sorted elements on 3 ranks, colored by rank. The first 19 mod 3 = 1 rank holds one more element. The balance takes three collective operations: `MPI_Allgather` for the counts, `MPI_Alltoall` for the send counts and `MPI_Alltoallv` for the elements. Adapted from: [An Efficient Parallel Load-balancing Framework for Orthogonal Decomposition of Geometrical Data](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-41321-1_5)

- **split**: `MPI_Comm_split` divides the ranks into $$S_k$$ groups of consecutive ranks, called subnetworks. Each subnetwork holds the objects between two cuts, which form one region, and runs the next round on it, independently of the others:

{: .picture}
 <img width="60.2%" height="60.2%" src="{{ site.assets }}/slicing/split.svg">

{: .caption}
The split of 8 ranks into $$S_k = 2$$ and $$S_k = 4$$ subnetworks of consecutive ranks, after the sort and the balance, colored by rank. The data stays in place, and each rank gets a new rank in its subnetwork. The split is one collective operation, `MPI_Comm_split`. $$S_k$$ divides the number of ranks. Adapted from: [An Efficient Parallel Load-balancing Framework for Orthogonal Decomposition of Geometrical Data](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-41321-1_5)

After $$d$$ rounds, each subnetwork is a single rank, which holds one slice. This gives SBS two advantages: its number of communication steps is fixed, since it runs $$d$$ rounds of a few collective operations whatever $$n$$, $$p$$ and the distribution of the data, and its cuts are exact, not approximate. In exchange, SBS moves the objects in every round, in the sort and in the balance, while the histogram and sampling methods compute all the cuts first and move each object only once, to its final rank. In other words, SBS trades communication for balance: it suits applications that need an exact balance, while a histogram or a sample costs less when an approximate balance is enough.
