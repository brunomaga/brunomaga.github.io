The [variational inference post]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2019-07-01-variational-inference-gmm %}) approximated an intractable posterior with a simpler, parametric distribution, found by maximizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO). Neural networks carry this idea to much more complex models, and they lead to two families of [generative models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_model), i.e. models that learn the distribution of the training data and can draw new samples from it: [variational autoencoders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variational_autoencoder) (VAEs) and [generative adversarial networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_adversarial_network) (GANs). A VAE maximizes the ELBO, as variational inference does. A GAN takes a different route: it trains a generator against a classifier, and it needs no likelihood at all.

## Variational Autoencoder (VAE)

The VAE was introduced in the paper [Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114) (Kingma and Welling, 2014), and the same authors cover it in detail in [An Introduction to Variational Autoencoders](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02691) (Kingma and Welling, 2019). This section summarizes both.

The papers address inference and learning on large datasets, in models whose latent variables have intractable posterior distributions. The model generates a data point $$x$$ in two steps: it first draws a latent variable $$z$$ from a prior $$p(z)$$, and then draws $$x$$ from a likelihood $$p_\theta(x \mid z)$$, which a neural network with parameters $$\theta$$, the **decoder** or generative model, computes from $$z$$. The prior and the likelihood are parametric and differentiable, but $$\theta$$ and the latent variable of each data point are unknown, and the problem is intractable in three ways:
- the evidence $$p_\theta(x) = \int p(z) \, p_\theta(x \mid z) \, dz$$ has no closed form, so it can be neither evaluated nor differentiated;
- the posterior $$p_\theta(z \mid x) = p_\theta(x \mid z) \, p(z) / p_\theta(x)$$ is intractable too, so the [expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expectation%E2%80%93maximization_algorithm) cannot be used;
- the expectations that a mean-field algorithm, such as CAVI, needs are intractable as well.

This level of intractability is common: a decoder with a single nonlinear hidden layer is enough to cause it.

The VAE solves these problems with a second neural network, the **encoder** or recognition model, which approximates the posterior with a distribution $$q_\phi(z \mid x)$$ of parameters $$\phi$$. Given a data point $$x$$, the encoder produces a distribution, for example a Gaussian, over the codes $$z$$ that could have generated $$x$$, and given a code $$z$$, the decoder produces a distribution over the possible values of $$x$$. The VAE is thus a probabilistic autoencoder, made of two coupled but independently parameterized models that are trained together. As in variational inference, training maximizes the ELBO, but with gradient ascent on $$\phi$$ and $$\theta$$ jointly, instead of the closed-form expectations of CAVI. The paper calls this algorithm **auto-encoding variational Bayes** (AEVB). It applies to datasets of independent samples with a continuous latent variable per sample, and it scales to large datasets, since it trains on mini-batches. Besides the estimation of $$\theta$$, it gives the approximate posterior of $$z$$ for any $$x$$, and an approximate marginal density of $$x$$, which serves tasks such as image denoising and inpainting.

The figure compares the two structures. A deterministic [autoencoder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoencoder) (top path) maps an input $$x$$ to a code $$z$$, and the code back to a reconstruction $$\hat{x}$$, so the code is both the output of the encoder and the input of the decoder. The encoder of a VAE (bottom path) instead outputs the parameters of the distribution of $$z$$, for example the mean $$\mu$$ and the variance $$\sigma^2$$ of a Gaussian, and the decoder receives a code drawn from that distribution.

This design has an important advantage over ordinary variational inference. The encoder computes the parameters of $$q_\phi(z \mid x)$$ for any input with one set of weights $$\phi$$, which is called **amortized inference**. In variational inference, by contrast, each data point has its own variational distribution: in the mixture model of the VI post, for example, each observation has its own assignment probabilities. The number of variational parameters thus grows with the dataset, which is inefficient for large datasets, and a new observation needs a new optimization, while the encoder of a VAE handles it with a single forward pass.

### Loss Function

The VAE is trained by maximizing the ELBO of each data point, so its loss is the negative ELBO. With the joint density $$p(z, x) = p(z) \, p_\theta(x \mid z)$$, the ELBO of a data point $$x$$ splits into two terms, as in the VI post:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; x) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\left[ \log p_\theta(x \mid z) \right] - \mathrm{KL}\left( q_\phi(z \mid x) \Vert p(z) \right) \le \log p_\theta(x)
$$

The training loss is $$-\mathcal{L}$$, summed over the data points, and each of its two terms has a clear role:
- The first term is the expected log-likelihood of $$x$$ given its code, over the codes that the encoder assigns to $$x$$. Its negative is a **reconstruction loss**, the loss of a regular autoencoder, which compares the output of the model with its input. Its form depends on the decoder: a Gaussian decoder with a fixed variance gives the squared error, and a Bernoulli decoder gives the binary cross-entropy.
- The second term is the KL divergence between the distribution of the encoder and the prior, usually the standard Gaussian $$p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$, and it penalizes the encoder when its codes do not follow the prior. For a Gaussian encoder with means $$\mu_j$$ and variances $$\sigma_j^2$$ in the $$J$$ latent dimensions, it has a closed form:

  $$
  \mathrm{KL}\left( q_\phi(z \mid x) \Vert p(z) \right) = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^J \left( 1 + \log \sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2 \right)
  $$

  Each term of the sum is zero for $$\mu_j = 0$$ and $$\sigma_j^2 = 1$$ and positive otherwise: it grows as $$\mu_j$$ moves away from 0, and without bound as $$\sigma_j^2$$ shrinks toward 0. The KL term thus pulls the codes toward the origin and keeps their variance from vanishing, which would turn the VAE back into a deterministic autoencoder. Since the codes of the data stay close to the prior, a code drawn from the prior decodes to a plausible sample: this is how a trained VAE generates new data.

### Reparameterization Trick

Training needs the gradient of the loss with respect to both $$\theta$$ and $$\phi$$. The gradient with respect to $$\theta$$ is simple to compute, because the expectation is over $$q_\phi$$, which does not depend on $$\theta$$. The gradient with respect to $$\phi$$ is harder: it is the gradient of an expectation over a distribution that itself depends on $$\phi$$. In other words, $$z$$ is drawn at random, and backpropagation cannot pass through a random draw. A general solution is the score-function estimator,

$$
\nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\left[ f(z) \, \nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z \mid x) \right]
$$

which only needs samples of $$z$$, but its variance is too high for training. The authors proposed instead the **reparameterization trick**, which separates the randomness from the parameters with a deterministic transformation. Instead of drawing $$z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$$, the trick writes the sample as:

$$
z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)
$$

where $$\mu$$ and $$\sigma$$ are outputs of the encoder and $$\odot$$ is the element-wise product. This works for two reasons. First, the randomness is now isolated in $$\epsilon$$, whose distribution does not depend on the parameters of the model. Second, $$z$$ is a differentiable function of $$\mu$$ and $$\sigma$$, and thus of $$\phi$$. The gradient can therefore move inside the expectation, and backpropagation computes it through $$z$$, as the figure shows:

$$
\nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\left[ \nabla_\phi f(\mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon) \right]
$$

The paper calls the resulting estimator of the ELBO **stochastic gradient variational Bayes** (SGVB). It is differentiable, so any gradient method can optimize it, and one sample of $$\epsilon$$ per data point is enough when the mini-batches hold 100 data points. A training step thus runs the encoder, draws $$\epsilon$$, runs the decoder and backpropagates through both networks, at about the cost of a training step of an autoencoder. On the MNIST and Frey Face datasets, AEVB converged considerably faster than the wake-sleep algorithm, and reached a better lower bound in all experiments.

## Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)

The GAN was introduced in the paper [Generative Adversarial Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661) (Goodfellow et al., 2014), which this section summarizes. The paper trains two models at the same time: a generative model G, which captures the distribution of the data, and a discriminative model D, which estimates the probability that a sample came from the training data rather than from G. G is trained to maximize the probability that D makes a mistake, so the two models play a game against each other, which gives the method its name. The authors explain it with an analogy: *"The generative model can be thought of as analogous to a team of counterfeiters, trying to produce fake currency and use it without detection, while the discriminative model is analogous to the police, trying to detect the counterfeit currency. Competition in this game drives both teams to improve their methods until the counterfeits are indistiguishable from the genuine articles."*

In more detail, the **generator** $$G(z; \theta_g)$$ is a neural network with parameters $$\theta_g$$, a [multilayer perceptron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilayer_perceptron) in the paper, which maps a noise vector $$z$$, drawn from a prior $$p_z(z)$$, to a sample $$G(z)$$. The samples follow a distribution $$p_g$$, which G defines only implicitly: it can draw samples of $$p_g$$, but it gives no explicit density. The goal of the generator is to make $$p_g$$ match the distribution of the data, well enough to fool the discriminator. The **discriminator** $$D(x; \theta_d)$$, a second network with parameters $$\theta_d$$, takes an input $$x$$ and outputs a single number, the probability that $$x$$ comes from the data rather than from $$p_g$$.

### Minimax Objective

The discriminator is trained to guess the source of its input as well as it can, i.e. to maximize the probability of assigning the correct label to both the training examples and the samples of G. The generator is trained at the same time to minimize $$\log(1 - D(G(z)))$$, i.e. to make its samples look real. D and G therefore play a two-player [minimax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimax) game with the value function:

$$
\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\left[ \log D(x) \right] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}\left[ \log\left( 1 - D(G(z)) \right) \right]
$$

The two terms cover the two kinds of input. The first term is largest when D recognizes the real inputs, drawn from the data, as real. The second term covers the fake inputs, generated from $$z \sim p_z$$, and on this term the two players have opposite goals: D maximizes it, to classify the fake inputs as fake, and G minimizes it, so that D classifies them as real. G only appears in this second term. Together, the two terms are the log-likelihood of D as a binary classifier, with the label 1 for the data and 0 for the generated samples, i.e. the negative of its binary [cross-entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-entropy) loss. The logarithm turns the product of the probabilities of the correct labels into a sum, which is faster and more stable to optimize, and since it is monotonically increasing, it does not move the optimum.

What does this game converge to? For a fixed G, the change of variables $$x = G(z)$$ turns the second expectation into an expectation over $$p_g$$, so that $$V$$ becomes a single integral over $$x$$:

$$
V(D, G) = \int \left( p_{\text{data}}(x) \log D(x) + p_g(x) \log\left( 1 - D(x) \right) \right) dx
$$

The function $$a \log y + b \log(1 - y)$$ has its maximum at $$y = a / (a + b)$$, so the optimal discriminator is $$D^*(x) = p_{\text{data}}(x) / (p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x))$$, which compares the density of the data with the density of the generator at $$x$$. With this discriminator, the objective of G becomes

$$
\max_D V(D, G) = -\log 4 + 2 \, \mathrm{JSD}(p_{\text{data}} \Vert p_g)
$$

where $$\mathrm{JSD}$$ is the [Jensen–Shannon divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jensen%E2%80%93Shannon_divergence), a symmetric measure of the difference between two distributions, which is zero only for $$p_g = p_{\text{data}}$$. The global optimum of the game is therefore reached when the generator reproduces the distribution of the data, $$p_g = p_{\text{data}}$$, and the discriminator can no longer tell the two apart, $$D^*(x) = 1/2$$ everywhere. Section 4 of the paper proves that training converges to this optimum, but the proof assumes that G and D have enough capacity and that D reaches its optimum at every step, which neural networks trained with stochastic gradient descent do not guarantee.

### Training

Optimizing D to completion before each update of G is, in the words of the paper, "computationally prohibitive, and on finite datasets would result in overfitting". Training instead alternates between $$k$$ gradient steps on D and one step on G, which keeps D near its optimum as long as G changes slowly enough. The expectations of the value function are estimated with averages over mini-batches, which approach the expectations as the mini-batches grow, by the [law of large numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_large_numbers). Each training iteration:

1. repeats $$k$$ times: draw $$m$$ noise vectors $$z^{(i)} \sim p_z$$ and $$m$$ data samples $$x^{(i)} \sim p_{\text{data}}$$, and update D by ascending the stochastic gradient

   $$
   \nabla_{\theta_d} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \left[ \log D\left( x^{(i)} \right) + \log\left( 1 - D\left( G\left( z^{(i)} \right) \right) \right) \right]
   $$

2. draws $$m$$ noise vectors $$z^{(i)} \sim p_z$$, and updates G by descending the stochastic gradient

   $$
   \nabla_{\theta_g} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \log\left( 1 - D\left( G\left( z^{(i)} \right) \right) \right)
   $$

The paper uses $$k = 1$$, the least expensive option, and gradient steps with momentum. The gradient of G passes through D, so a step of G runs the forward and the backward passes of both networks.

### Saturation of the Generator Loss

The value function has a practical problem: it may not give G enough gradient to learn. Early in training, G is poor, and D can reject its samples with high confidence, because they are clearly different from the training data. The term $$\log(1 - D(G(z)))$$ then saturates, i.e. it becomes flat. To see why, write the output of D as $$D = \sigma(a)$$, the [sigmoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmoid_function) of a logit $$a$$. The derivative of $$\log(1 - D)$$ with respect to $$a$$ is $$-D$$, which is close to 0 when D rejects a sample, so almost no gradient flows back to G. The updates of G are then tiny and training progresses slowly, and in low-precision arithmetic, such as 16-bit floating point, such small gradients can even underflow to zero. The paper therefore trains G to maximize $$\log D(G(z))$$ instead, whose derivative with respect to $$a$$ is $$1 - D$$, close to 1 when D rejects a sample. This **non-saturating loss** has the same fixed point of the dynamics of G and D, but it "provides much stronger gradients early in learning". The algorithm above shows the original, saturating update.

### Mode Collapse

A GAN should generate a variety of outputs, ideally a new valid sample for every random input of the generator. Sometimes, however, its outputs lack variety: they are a set of nearly identical samples. This failure is called **mode collapse**, and it happens when G maps many values of $$z$$ to the same output. The original paper mentions it briefly, as "the Helvetica scenario", which it avoids by not training G too much without updating D. [Durall et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.09673) describe it as follows: "This undesirable event occurs when the model can only fit a few modes of the data distribution, while ignoring the majority of them". They study it with the eigenvalues of the Hessian of the loss, and propose an optimizer that uses this second-order information. Mode collapse is an active field of research, with several other remedies: the [Wasserstein GAN](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.07875) changes the loss, [unrolled GANs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.02163.pdf) compute the loss of G against several future updates of D, so that G cannot over-optimize for a single discriminator, and [conditional GANs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.1784) condition both networks on labels. Likelihood-based models, such as the VAE and the [VQ-VAE](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00937), are less prone to mode collapse. They maximize the likelihood of the data, or a bound of it, which minimizes $$\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \Vert p_\theta)$$ or an upper bound of it, and this divergence penalizes a model that gives a low probability to a mode of the data (see the [statistics post]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2018-06-15-statistics-for-ml %}#cross-entropy-and-kl-divergence)).

## VAE or GAN

The two models reach the same goal, a generator of new samples, by different routes, and the table compares them.

| | VAE | GAN |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | one loss: the negative ELBO | a minimax game between two networks |
| Training | stable | alternating updates that can oscillate or collapse to a few modes |
| Latent code of an input | given by the encoder | not available |
| Likelihood of an input | lower bound (ELBO) | not available |
| Samples | lower quality, often blurry with a Gaussian decoder | sharp |

The samples of a VAE are often blurry because, with a Gaussian decoder, the squared error is smallest for the average of the plausible outputs, which is a blurred image. The discriminator of a GAN, by contrast, learns to reject samples that do not look like the data. A VAE therefore suits tasks that need the likelihood or the latent code of an input, and a GAN suits tasks that need sharp samples. Both keep two networks in memory during training. [Diffusion models]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2025-02-25-diffusion %}) also train on a variational bound, and reach the sample quality of GANs.
