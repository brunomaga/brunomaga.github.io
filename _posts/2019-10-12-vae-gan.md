[Variational autoencoders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variational_autoencoder) (VAEs) and [generative adversarial networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_adversarial_network) (GANs) are [generative models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_model) built from neural networks: they learn the distribution of the training data and draw new samples from it. A VAE maximizes the evidence lower bound (ELBO) of the [variational inference post]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2019-07-01-variational-inference-gmm %}). A GAN trains a generator against a classifier and needs no likelihood.

## Variational Autoencoder (VAE)

The VAE was introduced in [Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114) (Kingma and Welling, 2014), and [An Introduction to Variational Autoencoders](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02691) (Kingma and Welling, 2019) covers it in detail.

The model generates a data point $$x$$ in two steps: it draws a latent variable $$z$$ from a prior $$p(z)$$, then draws $$x$$ from a likelihood $$p_\theta(x \mid z)$$ computed by a neural network with parameters $$\theta$$, the **decoder**. With a nonlinear decoder, the evidence $$p_\theta(x) = \int p(z) \, p_\theta(x \mid z) \, dz$$, the posterior $$p_\theta(z \mid x)$$ and the expectations of mean-field VI are all intractable, so neither the [EM algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expectation%E2%80%93maximization_algorithm) nor CAVI applies. The VAE approximates the posterior with $$q_\phi(z \mid x)$$, the **encoder**, a second neural network with parameters $$\phi$$. Gradient ascent on the ELBO learns $$\phi$$ jointly with $$\theta$$, instead of computing closed-form expectations as CAVI does. The paper calls this algorithm **auto-encoding variational Bayes** (AEVB). It applies to datasets of independent samples with a continuous latent variable per sample, and scales to large datasets.

A deterministic [autoencoder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoencoder) maps the input $$x$$ to a code $$z$$, and the code back to a reconstruction of $$x$$. The encoder of a VAE instead outputs the parameters of the distribution of $$z$$, e.g. the mean $$\mu$$ and the variance $$\sigma^2$$ of a Gaussian, and the decoder receives a sample of that distribution:

The encoder computes the parameters of $$q_\phi(z \mid x)$$ for any input with one set of weights $$\phi$$, which is called **amortized inference**. In the mixture model of the VI post, each observation has its own variational parameters, so their number grows with the dataset, and a new observation needs a new optimization. The VAE encoder handles a new observation with one forward pass.

### Loss Function

With $$p(z, x) = p(z) \, p_\theta(x \mid z)$$, the ELBO of a data point $$x$$ splits into two terms:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; x) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\left[ \log p_\theta(x \mid z) \right] - \mathrm{KL}\left( q_\phi(z \mid x) \Vert p(z) \right) \le \log p_\theta(x)
$$

The training loss is $$-\mathcal{L}$$, summed over the data points:
- The first term is the expected log-likelihood of $$x$$ given its code, so its negative is a **reconstruction loss**. A Gaussian decoder with a fixed variance gives the squared error, and a Bernoulli decoder gives the binary cross-entropy.
- The second term keeps $$q_\phi(z \mid x)$$ close to the prior. For the prior $$p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$ and a Gaussian encoder with means $$\mu_j$$ and variances $$\sigma_j^2$$ in the $$J$$ latent dimensions, it has a closed form:

  $$
  \mathrm{KL}\left( q_\phi(z \mid x) \Vert p(z) \right) = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^J \left( 1 + \log \sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2 \right)
  $$

  Since the codes of the data stay close to the prior, a code drawn from the prior decodes to a plausible sample: this is how a trained VAE generates data.

### Reparameterization Trick

The gradient of $$\mathcal{L}$$ with respect to $$\phi$$ is the gradient of an expectation over $$q_\phi$$, and the sampling of $$z$$ is not differentiable. The score-function estimator, $$\nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f(z) \, \nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z \mid x)]$$, has a variance too high for training. The **reparameterization trick** writes the sample as a differentiable function of $$\phi$$ and of a noise variable $$\epsilon$$ whose distribution does not depend on $$\phi$$. For the Gaussian encoder, $$z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$$ with $$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$, where $$\mu$$ and $$\sigma$$ are outputs of the encoder and $$\odot$$ is the element-wise product. The gradient then moves inside the expectation, $$\nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}[\nabla_\phi f(\mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon)]$$, and backpropagation computes it through $$z$$:

The variational parameters <b><i>ϕ</i></b> affect the objective <i>f</i> through the random variable <b>z</b> ~ <i>q</i><sub><b><i>ϕ</i></b></sub>(<b>z</b>\|<b>x</b>). We wish to compute gradients ∇<sub><b><i>ϕ</i></b></sub><i>f</i> to optimize the objective with SGD. In the original form (left), we cannot differentiate <i>f</i> w.r.t. <b><i>ϕ</i></b>, because we cannot directly backpropagate gradients through the random variable <b>z</b>. We can 'externalize' the randomness in <b>z</b> by re-parameterizing the variable as a deterministic and differentiable function of <b><i>ϕ</i></b>, <b>x</b>, and a newly introduced random variable <b><i>ε</i></b>. This allows us to 'backprop through <b>z</b>', and compute gradients ∇<sub><b><i>ϕ</i></b></sub><i>f</i>.

The paper calls the resulting estimator of the ELBO **stochastic gradient variational Bayes** (SGVB). One sample of $$\epsilon$$ per data point suffices with mini-batches of 100 data points. A training step thus runs the encoder, draws $$\epsilon$$, runs the decoder and backpropagates through both networks, at the cost of a training step of an autoencoder. On the MNIST and Frey Face datasets, AEVB converged faster and to a higher lower bound than the wake-sleep algorithm.

## Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)

The GAN was introduced in [Generative Adversarial Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661) (Goodfellow et al., 2014). A **generator** $$G(z; \theta_g)$$, a neural network with parameters $$\theta_g$$, maps a noise vector $$z$$, drawn from a prior $$p_z(z)$$, to a sample $$G(z)$$. The samples follow a distribution $$p_g$$ that has no explicit density. A **discriminator** $$D(x; \theta_d)$$, a second neural network, outputs the probability that $$x$$ comes from the data rather than from $$p_g$$. D learns to tell the data from the generated samples, and G learns to fool D. The authors compare G to counterfeiters and D to the police: *"Competition in this game drives both teams to improve their methods until the counterfeits are indistiguishable from the genuine articles."*

### Minimax Objective

D and G play a two-player [minimax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimax) game with the value function

$$
\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\left[ \log D(x) \right] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}\left[ \log\left( 1 - D(G(z)) \right) \right]
$$

$$V$$ is the log-likelihood of D as a binary classifier, with the label 1 for the data and 0 for the generated samples, i.e. the negative of its binary [cross-entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-entropy) loss. D maximizes $$V$$, and G minimizes the second term, the only one that depends on G.

For a fixed G, the optimal discriminator is $$D^*(x) = p_{\text{data}}(x) / (p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x))$$. With it, the objective of G becomes

$$
\max_D V(D, G) = -\log 4 + 2 \, \mathrm{JSD}(p_{\text{data}} \Vert p_g)
$$

where $$\mathrm{JSD}$$ is the [Jensen–Shannon divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jensen%E2%80%93Shannon_divergence), which is zero only for $$p_g = p_{\text{data}}$$. The global optimum of the game is thus $$p_g = p_{\text{data}}$$, with $$D^*(x) = 1/2$$ everywhere. The proof that training converges to it (section 4 of the paper) assumes that G and D have enough capacity and that D reaches its optimum at every step, which neural networks trained with SGD do not guarantee.

### Training

Optimizing D to completion before each update of G is "computationally prohibitive, and on finite datasets would result in overfitting". Training instead alternates $$k$$ gradient steps on D with one step on G, which keeps D near its optimum as long as G changes slowly. Each training iteration:

1. repeats $$k$$ times: draw $$m$$ noise vectors $$z^{(i)} \sim p_z$$ and $$m$$ data samples $$x^{(i)} \sim p_{\text{data}}$$, and update D by ascending the stochastic gradient

   $$
   \nabla_{\theta_d} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \left[ \log D\left( x^{(i)} \right) + \log\left( 1 - D\left( G\left( z^{(i)} \right) \right) \right) \right]
   $$

2. draws $$m$$ noise vectors $$z^{(i)} \sim p_z$$, and updates G by descending the stochastic gradient

   $$
   \nabla_{\theta_g} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \log\left( 1 - D\left( G\left( z^{(i)} \right) \right) \right)
   $$

The paper uses $$k = 1$$, the least expensive option, and gradient steps with momentum. The gradient of G passes through D, so a step of G runs the forward and backward passes of both networks.

### Saturation of the Generator Loss

Early in training, D rejects the samples of G with high confidence, and $$\log(1 - D(G(z)))$$ saturates. With $$D = \sigma(a)$$, the [sigmoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmoid_function) of the logit $$a$$ that D computes, the derivative of $$\log(1 - D)$$ with respect to $$a$$ is $$-D$$, close to 0 when D rejects a sample, so G receives almost no gradient. The paper thus trains G to maximize $$\log D(G(z))$$ instead, whose derivative with respect to $$a$$ is $$1 - D$$, close to 1. This **non-saturating loss** has the same fixed point of the dynamics of G and D, and stronger gradients early in training. The algorithm above shows the original, saturating update.

### Mode Collapse

In **mode collapse**, G maps many values of $$z$$ to the same output, and the samples cover only a few modes of the data distribution. The paper mentions it briefly as "the Helvetica scenario", which it avoids by not training G too much without updating D. [Durall et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.09673) study it with the eigenvalues of the Hessian of the loss, and propose an optimizer that uses this second-order information. Other remedies change the loss, as the [Wasserstein GAN](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.07875) does, compute the loss of G against several future updates of D, as [unrolled GANs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.02163.pdf) do, or condition both networks on labels, as [conditional GANs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.1784) do. Likelihood-based models, such as the VAE and the [VQ-VAE](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00937), maximize the likelihood of the data or a bound of it. This minimizes $$\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \Vert p_\theta)$$, which penalizes a model that gives a low probability to a mode of the data (see the [statistics post]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2018-06-15-statistics-for-ml %}#cross-entropy-and-kl-divergence)).

## VAE or GAN

| | VAE | GAN |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | one loss: the negative ELBO | a minimax game between two networks |
| Training | stable | alternating updates that can oscillate or collapse to a few modes |
| Latent code of an input | given by the encoder | not available |
| Likelihood of an input | lower bound (ELBO) | not available |
| Samples | lower quality, often blurry with a Gaussian decoder | sharp |

A VAE suits tasks that need the likelihood or the latent code of an input, and a GAN suits tasks that need sharp samples. Both keep two networks in memory. [Diffusion models]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2025-02-25-diffusion %}) also train on a variational bound, and reach the sample quality of GANs.
