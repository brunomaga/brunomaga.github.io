This post covers two methods for generative ML on non-parametric distributions, based on neural networks: Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) and Generative Adversarial Neural Networks (GANs).


## Variational Autoencoder (VAE)

Credit: most of this section summarizes the papers [Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114) and [An Introduction to Variational Autoencoders](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02691) by Diederik P. Kingma and Max Welling at the Universiteit van Amsterdam. 

The papers introduce Variational Autoencoders for efficient inference and learning in probabilistic models whose latent variables and parameters have intractable posterior distributions, and with large datasets. The proposed SGVB (Stochastic Gradient Variational Bayes) estimator efficiently approximates posterior inference in almost any model with continuous latent variables. Because it is differentiable, it can be optimized with e.g. gradient descent. The proposed Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes (AEVB) algorithm uses the SGVB estimator to optimize a model that performs efficient approximate posterior inference using simple sampling (from parametric distributions).

The VAE is a probabilistic auto-encoder, where the probabilistic encoder (aka recognition model) and decoder (aka generative model) are neural networks. AEVB is a learning/inference algorithm that finds the parameters of the VAE, i.e. it performs approximate variational inference in the directed graphical model of the VAE.

Input datapoints $$x$$ are generated as follows:
- a value $$z(i)$$ is generated from some prior distribution $$p_{\theta^*}(z)$$; and
- a value $$x(i)$$ is generated from the likelihood distribution $$p_{\theta^∗}(x \mid z)$$.

Both the prior and likelihood functions are assumed to be parametric differentiable distributions, and $$\theta^*$$ and $$z(i)$$ are unknown. The problem is also assumed to be intractable:
- the integral of the evidence or marginal likelihood $$\int p_\theta(z) p_\theta(x \mid z) dz$$ is intractable;
- the true posterior density $$p_\theta (z \mid x)$$ is intractable, so the expectation maximization algorithm cannot be used; and
- the integrals required by any mean-field approximation algorithm are also intractable.
This level of intractability is common in DNNs with nonlinear hidden layers.

The paper proposes efficient approximations for the MAP estimation of the parameters $$\theta$$, the posterior inference of the latent variable $$z$$, and the marginal inference of the variable $$x$$. As in other variational inference methods, **the intractable true posterior $$p_{\theta}(z \mid x)$$ is approximated by $$q_\phi(z  \mid  x)$$ (the Encoder)**. However, its parameters $$\phi$$ are computed by the Encoder DNN and not by a closed-form expectation. $$p_\theta(x \mid z)$$ is the Decoder: given a $$z$$, it generates the output, a distribution over the possible values of $$x$$. Given a datapoint $$x$$, the encoder produces a distribution (e.g. a Gaussian) over the possible values of the code $$z$$ from which the datapoint $$x$$ could have been generated.

### Non-Variational vs Variational AutoEncoders

The proposed VAE has a DNN decoder and a DNN encoder, with parameters $$\theta$$ and $$\phi$$ optimized jointly by the AEVB algorithm, where $$p_\theta(x \mid z)$$ is a Gaussian/Bernoulli whose distribution parameters are computed from $$z$$. The VAE can therefore be viewed as two coupled, *independently* parameterized models, trained together: the encoder/recognition model and the decoder/generative model. The encoder delivers to the decoder an approximation to its posterior over latent random variables.

Variational Autoencoders differ from regular Autoencoders (such as sequence-to-sequence encoder-decoders) as follows:
- in regular AEs, the latent representation $$z$$ is both the output of the encoder and the input of the decoder;
- in VAEs, the encoder outputs the parameters of the distribution of $$z$$ -- e.g. the mean and variance of a Gaussian distribution. $$z$$ is then drawn from the distribution with those parameters and passed to the decoder;

### Why VAE instead of Variational Inference

One advantage of the VAE framework over ordinary Variational Inference is that the encoder is a stochastic function of the input variables. In VI, each data-case has a separate variational distribution, which *is inefficient for large datasets*.

### The reparametrization Trick

The authors noticed that sampling induces noise in the gradients required for learning (or, put differently, that $$z$$ is randomly generated and cannot be back-propagated). Randomness disrupts the deterministic computation of gradients, so the model cannot be optimized directly via gradient descent. To solve this, the authors propose the “reparameterization trick”.

The reparameterization trick decouples the sampling process from the stochasticity by introducing a deterministic transformation. Instead of sampling $$z \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$$, $$z$$ is rewritten as $$ z = \mu + \sigma \cdot \epsilon$$, where $$\epsilon$$ follows the standard Gaussian, i.e. $$\epsilon \sim N(0,1)$$.

This works for two reasons:
- The randomness is now isolated in $$𝜖$$, **which does not depend on the model parameters**.
- The computation of $$𝑧$$ becomes a differentiable function of $$𝜇$$ and $$𝜎$$. Gradients flow through $$𝜇$$ and $$𝜎$$ during backpropagation, because **the transformation 
$$𝑧 = 𝜇 + 𝜎 ⋅ 𝜖$$ is fully differentiable with respect to these parameters**.

### The loss function

The loss function is the negative of the evidence lower bound (ELBO) below, i.e. the sum of two terms:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(\mathbf{z} \vert \mathbf{x})} [\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x} \vert \mathbf{z})] - D_{KL} \big( q_\phi(\mathbf{z} \vert \mathbf{x}) \parallel p(\mathbf{z}) \big)
$$

The first term is the reconstruction loss (or expected negative log-likelihood of the i-th datapoint). The expectation is taken with respect to the encoder’s distribution over the representations, which encourages the VAE to generate valid datapoints. This loss compares the model output with the model input and can be any loss used in regular autoencoders, such as the L2 loss, cross-entropy, etc.

The second term is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the encoder’s distribution $$q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$$ and the standard Gaussian $$p(z)$$, where $$p(z)=\mathcal{N}(\mu=0, \sigma^2=1)$$. This divergence compares the latent vector with a zero-mean, unit-variance Gaussian distribution. It penalizes the VAE if it produces latent vectors that are not from the desired distribution, i.e. if the encoder outputs representations $$z$$ that differ from those of a standard normal distribution. This regularizer term means ‘keep the representations $$z$$ of each digit sufficiently diverse’.

### Results

The paper tested the VAE on the MNIST and Frey Face datasets and compared the variational lower bound and estimated marginal likelihood, showing improved results over the wake-sleep algorithm.

### Detour: why maximize the Expected Value?

In the Bayesian setup, the loss function is often the sum of the expected values of several terms.

The reason is the [Law of large numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_large_numbers). According to this law, the average of the outcomes of a large number of trials should be close to the expected value, and it gets closer to the expected value as the number of trials increases.

Consider a game (Blackjack, Slots, Rock-Paper-Scissors) that can be repeated as many times as desired. By the law of large numbers, the value of the wins over $$n$$ iterations of the game approximates $$n \mathbb{E}(X)$$. The problem is therefore optimised according to the average outcome of the experiment over many repetitions, i.e. by maximizing its expected value. The expected value is not necessarily the most likely outcome.

Over the long run, the law of large numbers often guarantees a better outcome, and this outcome is maximized by maximizing the *expected* outcome of the optimisation.


## Generative Adversarial Neural Network (GAN)

Credit: most of this section summarizes the paper [Generative Adversarial Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661), published at NeurIPS 2014, by Ian Goodfellow and colleagues at the University of Montreal.

The paper introduces a new generative model composed of two models trained simultaneously:
- a generative model G that captures the data distribution; and
- a discriminative model D that estimates the probability that a sample came from the training data rather than G;

G is trained to maximize the probability of D making a mistake. This framework corresponds to a minimax two-player game. The name *adversarial* comes from the generative model facing the discriminative model, which learns whether a sample is from the model distribution or from the data distribution. Quoting the authors: *"The generative model can be thought of as analogous to a team of counterfeiters, trying to produce fake currency and use it without detection, while the discriminative model is analogous to the police, trying to detect the counterfeit currency. Competition in this game drives both teams to improve their methods until the counterfeits are indistiguishable from the genuine articles."*

### Mathematical setup

The generative model generates samples by passing random noise $$z$$ through a multilayer perceptron.
- The generator $$ G(z \mid \theta_g)$$ is a multilayer perceptron with parameters $$\theta_g$$.
- $$p_z(z)$$ is the prior on the input noise variables.
- The generator $$G$$ implicitly defines a probability distribution $$p_g$$ as the distribution of the samples $$G(z)$$ obtained when $$z \sim p_z$$;
- The goal of the generator is to learn the distribution $$p_g$$ over data $$x$$, in order to fake it "well enough" and trick the discriminator;

The discriminative model is also a multilayer perceptron.
- The discriminator $$D(x \mid \theta_d)$$ takes the input $$x$$ and, based on its DNN parameters $$\theta_d$$, outputs a single scalar representing the probability that $$x$$ came from the data rather than $$p_g$$;

### Loss and minimax challenge

The discriminator $$D$$ is trained to best guess the source of the data, i.e. to maximize the probability of assigning the correct label to both the training examples and the samples from $$G$$. 

The generator $$G$$ is trained simultaneously to minimize $$1-D(G(z))$$, i.e. to fake the data well enough. $$D$$ and $$G$$ therefore play a two-player minimax game with value function $$V(G,D)$$:

$$
\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x} \sim p_{\text{data}}(\boldsymbol{x})} [\log D(\boldsymbol{x})] + \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z} \sim p_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{z})} [\log (1 - D(G(\boldsymbol{z})))].
$$

The loss function is a sum of two penalization terms:
- the first term optimises the discriminator such that, in the long run, all real inputs (drawn from the data, i.e. $$x \sim p_{data}$$) are expected to be as correct as possible;
- the second term covers the fake inputs (generated by the generator from $$z \sim p_z$$). The discriminator and the generator have opposite goals on this term: the discriminator maximizes it, to classify fake inputs as fake, and the generator minimizes it, so that the discriminator classifies fake inputs as real;

Finally, note the $$\log$$ term in both expected value terms. The objective function is a product of multiple probabilities, in this case $$D(x) * (1-D(G(z)))$$. With the $$\log$$, the optimisation returns the same optima --- as $$\log$$ is monotonic --- while being faster and more numerically stable. The expectation term is added to the loss because the goal is to minimize the expected loss over several samples (law of large numbers). Thus:

$$
\mathbb{E} \left[ \log(D(x) * (1-D(G(z)))) \right] = \mathbb{E} \left[ \log(D(x)) \right] + \mathbb{E} \left[ \log(1-D(G(z))) \right]
$$
 
In section 4 (theoretical results), the authors show that, given enough capacity for the discriminator and the generator, the data-generating distribution can be recovered. In practice, they show that this minimax game has a global optimum for $$p_g = p_{data}$$, and therefore the loss function can be optimized.


### Challenge: different convergence speed in the optimization of Generator and Discriminator

Optimizing $$D$$ to completion before each step of $$G$$ is "computationally prohibitive, and on finite datasets would result in overfitting". Instead, training alternates between $$k$$ steps of optimizing $$D$$ and one step of optimizing $$G$$ (the authors use $$k=1$$). This keeps $$D$$ near its optimal solution, as long as $$G$$ changes slowly enough.

### Challenge: saturation of discriminator loss

The loss equation may not provide sufficient gradient for $$G$$ to learn well. Early in learning, when $$G$$ is poor, $$D$$ can reject samples with high confidence because they are clearly different from the training data. In this case, $$\log(1 − D(G(z)))$$ saturates. Saturation means that $$D(G(z))$$ is close to 0, where this term is flat: its gradient with respect to the generator's parameters vanishes. This term of the loss function then provides no signal (gradients) for the update of the generator, making it useless. In other words, when the discriminator performs significantly better than the generator, the updates to the generator are either inaccurate or vanish.

This leads to two main problems:
- mathematically, the parameter updates are zero or very small, so progress through the loss landscape is very slow;
- computationally, arithmetic operations over very small values lead to incorrect (or "always zero") results due to insufficient floating-point precision in the processor (typically 16 or 32 bits); 

The authors suggest a different training objective for the generator: instead of minimizing $$\log(1 − D(G(z)))$$, the generator maximizes $$\log D(G(z))$$, so that the optimisation landscape "provides much stronger gradients early in learning". The pseudocode at the end of this post shows the original update, which minimizes $$\log(1 − D(G(z)))$$.


### Challenge: Mode collapse

The original publication mentions this challenge only briefly, as "the Helvetica scenario" in which $$G$$ collapses too many values of $$z$$ to the same value of $$x$$. A GAN should generate a range of outputs, ideally a new random valid output for every random input to the generator. Instead, **the output is sometimes monotonous, i.e. a range of very similar outputs**. This phenomenon is called Mode Collapse and is [detailed in this paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.09673) as: "This event occurs when the model can only fit a few modes of the data distribution, while ignoring the majority of them". That paper proposes a workaround using second-order gradient information. Mode collapse is an active field of research and several other solutions have been proposed, such as: a different loss function (Wasserstein loss), [Unrolled GANs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.02163.pdf), which incorporate the current and future discriminator's classifications in the loss function (so that the generator can't over-optimize for a single discriminator), [Conditional GANs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.1784), [VQ-VAEs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.01987), etc.

### Training algorithm

The final training algorithm is the following:

$$
\begin{array}{l}
\hline
\textbf{Algorithm 1} \text{ Minibatch stochastic gradient descent training of generative} \\
\text{adversarial nets. The number of steps to apply to the discriminator, } k \text{, is a} \\
\text{hyperparameter. We used } k = 1 \text{, the least expensive option, in our experiments.} \\
\hline
\quad \textbf{for} \text{ number of training iterations } \textbf{do} \\
\qquad \textbf{for}\ k \text{ steps } \textbf{do} \\
\qquad\quad \bullet \text{ Sample minibatch of } m \text{ noise samples } \{\boldsymbol{z}^{(1)}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{z}^{(m)}\} \text{ from noise prior } p_g(\boldsymbol{z}). \\
\qquad\quad \bullet \text{ Sample minibatch of } m \text{ examples } \{\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{x}^{(m)}\} \text{ from data generating distribution} \\
\qquad\quad p_{\text{data}}(\boldsymbol{x}). \\
\qquad\quad \bullet \text{ Update the discriminator by ascending its stochastic gradient:} \\
\hspace{9em} \displaystyle \nabla_{\theta_d} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \left[ \log D\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\right) + \log\left(1 - D\left(G\left(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\right)\right)\right) \right]. \\
\qquad \textbf{end for} \\
\qquad \bullet \text{ Sample minibatch of } m \text{ noise samples } \{\boldsymbol{z}^{(1)}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{z}^{(m)}\} \text{ from noise prior } p_g(\boldsymbol{z}). \\
\qquad \bullet \text{ Update the generator by descending its stochastic gradient:} \\
\hspace{12.8em} \displaystyle \nabla_{\theta_g} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \log\left(1 - D\left(G\left(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\right)\right)\right). \\
\quad \textbf{end for} \\
\text{The gradient-based updates can use any standard gradient-based learning rule.} \\
\text{We used momentum in our experiments.} \\
\hline
\end{array}
$$
