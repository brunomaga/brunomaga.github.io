In the early 2010s, the most striking successes of deep learning were discriminative models, such as classifiers. Deep [generative models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_model) had less impact, as their training by maximum likelihood involves many intractable probabilistic computations. A generative model with [latent variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latent_and_observable_variables) explains each datapoint $$x$$ by a hidden code $$z$$, drawn from a prior $$p(z)$$, so the likelihood of $$x$$ is an integral over all codes, $$p_\theta(x) = \int p(z) \, p_\theta(x \mid z) \, dz$$. When $$p_\theta(x \mid z)$$ is a neural network with parameters $$\theta$$, this integral has no closed form, and a numerical integration does not scale: a grid of only 10 points per dimension of a 20-dimensional code has $$10^{20}$$ points, each one a pass through the network.

Two papers proposed ways around this: [Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114) in 2013, which introduced the Variational Autoencoder (VAE), and [Generative Adversarial Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661) in 2014, which introduced the Generative Adversarial Network (GAN). The VAE maximizes a lower bound of the log-likelihood that can be estimated with one sample of $$z$$ per datapoint, while the GAN does without a likelihood: its generator learns from a second network, trained to tell real data from generated data.

In the quest to understand generative models in Machine Learning, a previous post looked into [Variational Inference]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2019-07-01-variational-inference-gmm %}) as a method to approximate an intractable posterior distribution by a simpler, parametric one. This post continues the quest and looks at two new methods based on neural networks for distributions that no standard parametric family describes: VAEs and GANs. It presents the structure and the loss function of each model, the reparameterization trick of the VAE and the challenges of training a GAN, and ends with a comparison of the two.

## Variational Autoencoder (VAE)

Credit: most content in this section is a summary of the papers [Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114), by Diederik P. Kingma and Max Welling at the Universiteit van Amsterdam, and [An Introduction to Variational Autoencoders](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02691), by the same authors. 

The papers introduce Variational Autoencoders, to perform efficient inference and learning in probabilistic models whose latent variables and parameters have intractable posterior distributions, on large datasets. The **Stochastic Gradient Variational Bayes (SGVB)** estimator of the variational lower bound (the ELBO, below) can be used for efficient approximation of posterior inference in almost any model with continuous latent variables, and, because it is differentiable, it can be optimized with stochastic gradient descent. The **Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes (AEVB)** algorithm uses the SGVB estimator to optimize a model that does efficient approximate posterior inference using simple sampling (from parametric distributions).

### Autoencoders and variational autoencoders

An [autoencoder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoencoder) (AE) is a neural network trained to reproduce its input: an **encoder** maps the input $$x$$ to a code $$z$$, usually of fewer dimensions, and a **decoder** maps the code back to a reconstruction $$\hat{x}$$ of the input. The loss compares $$\hat{x}$$ with $$x$$, e.g. with the squared error, so that the code keeps the information needed to rebuild the input.

The VAE is a probabilistic autoencoder, where the probabilistic encoder $$q_\phi(z \mid x)$$ (aka recognition model) and decoder $$p_\theta(x \mid z)$$ (aka generative model) are represented as neural networks, with parameters $$\phi$$ and $$\theta$$. The decoder is a Gaussian or a Bernoulli distribution, whose parameters are computed from $$z$$. Therefore, the VAE can be viewed as two coupled, *independently* parameterized models, trained together, where the encoder delivers to the decoder an approximation of its posterior over the latent variables. The AEVB algorithm finds the parameters of both, i.e. it performs approximate variational inference in the [directed graphical model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_network) of the VAE.

The difference between Variational Autoencoders and regular Autoencoders (and other encoder-decoders, such as sequence-to-sequence models) is that:
- in regular AEs, the latent representation $$z$$ is both the output of the encoder and the input of the decoder;
- in VAEs, the outputs of the encoder are the parameters of the distribution of $$z$$ (e.g. the mean and variance in the case of a Gaussian distribution), and $$z$$ is drawn from the distribution with those parameters and then passed to the decoder;

### The latent variable model

The VAE assumes that each datapoint $$x^{(i)}$$ of a dataset of [i.i.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically_distributed_random_variables) samples is generated by a random process of two steps, which involves an unobserved continuous random variable $$z$$:
- a value $$z^{(i)}$$ is generated from some prior distribution $$p_{\theta^*}(z)$$; and
- a value $$x^{(i)}$$ is generated from the likelihood distribution $$p_{\theta^*}(x \mid z)$$.

Think of $$z$$ as the hidden factors of a datapoint: for an image of a handwritten digit, which digit it is, its slant and the thickness of its stroke. The likelihood then draws the pixels of the image from these factors.

The assumptions are that both the prior and likelihood functions are parametric differentiable distributions, and that the true parameters $$\theta^*$$ and the latent values $$z^{(i)}$$ are unknown. There is also an assumption of intractability, as in:
- the integral of the evidence or marginal likelihood $$p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(z) \, p_\theta(x \mid z) \, dz$$ is intractable, so it can be neither evaluated nor differentiated;
- the true posterior density $$p_\theta(z \mid x) = p_\theta(x \mid z) \, p_\theta(z) / p_\theta(x)$$ is intractable, as it divides by this integral, so the [expectation maximization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expectation%E2%80%93maximization_algorithm) algorithm cannot be used; and
- the integrals required by any mean-field approximation algorithm are also intractable.

This is the level of intractability common to DNNs with nonlinear hidden layers.

### The encoder and amortized inference

The paper proposes an efficient approximation of the [Maximum Likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood_estimation) (MLE) or [Maximum a Posteriori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_a_posteriori_estimation) (MAP) estimation of the parameters $$\theta$$, of the posterior inference of the latent variable $$z$$, and of the marginal inference of the variable $$x$$. Similarly to other variational inference methods, **the intractable true posterior $$p_{\theta}(z \mid x)$$ is approximated by $$q_\phi(z \mid x)$$ (the encoder)**, whose parameters $$\phi$$ are not computed by a closed-form expectation but learned as the weights of the encoder network instead. Given a datapoint $$x$$, the encoder produces a distribution over the possible values of the code $$z$$ from which the datapoint $$x$$ could have been generated.

The VAE of the paper takes the prior as the centered isotropic Gaussian $$p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$, which has no parameters, and the encoder as a Gaussian with a diagonal covariance, $$q_\phi(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2))$$. The mean $$\mu$$ and the standard deviation $$\sigma$$, two vectors of the size of $$z$$, are the outputs of the encoder network for the input $$x$$ ($$\mu_z$$ and $$\Sigma_z = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2)$$ in the picture above).

One advantage of the VAE framework, relative to ordinary Variational Inference, is that the encoder is now a stochastic function of the input variables. In VI, each data-case has a separate variational distribution, which *is inefficient for large datasets*. For the 60,000 training images of [MNIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MNIST_database) and a 20-dimensional $$z$$, a mean and a variance per dimension make 2.4 million variational parameters, and a new image needs an optimization of its own. The encoder uses one set of parameters $$\phi$$ for all datapoints, and computes the distribution of a new datapoint with one forward pass, which is called **amortized inference**.

### The loss function: the ELBO

The VAE maximizes the [evidence lower bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence_lower_bound) (ELBO) of each datapoint, so its loss function is the negative of the ELBO. The ELBO is an expectation over the distribution of the encoder, which splits into the sum of two terms (Eq. 2 and 3 in the AEVB paper):

$$
\begin{align*}
\text{ELBO}(\theta, \phi; x) & = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)} \left[ \log p_\theta(x, z) - \log q_\phi(z \mid x) \right] \\
& = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)} \left[ \log p_\theta(x \mid z) \right] - \text{KL} \big( q_\phi(z \mid x) \parallel p(z) \big)
\end{align*}
$$

The first term is the expected log-likelihood of the datapoint, and its negative is the **reconstruction loss** (the paper calls this term an expected negative reconstruction error). The expectation is taken with respect to the encoder's distribution over the representations, encouraging the VAE to generate valid datapoints. This loss compares the model output with the model input. It can be any of the losses used in regular autoencoders, such as the L2 loss (for a Gaussian decoder with a fixed variance), the [cross-entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-entropy) (for a Bernoulli decoder), etc.

The second term is the [Kullback-Leibler divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kullback%E2%80%93Leibler_divergence) (KL) between the encoder's distribution $$q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$$ and the prior $$p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$, a zero-mean, unit-variance Gaussian. This divergence acts as a regularizer, as the paper calls it: it penalizes the VAE if the encoder outputs representations $$z$$ that are different from those of a standard normal distribution. Without it, the encoder could give each datapoint a representation in a different region of the latent space. For a VAE trained on images of handwritten digits, this regularizer term means ‘keep the representations $$z$$ of each digit sufficiently diverse’ (source: [Tutorial - What is a variational autoencoder?](https://jaan.io/what-is-variational-autoencoder-vae-tutorial/), by Jaan Altosaar).

With a Gaussian encoder and a Gaussian prior, this KL divergence has a closed form (Appendix B of the paper), so it needs no sampling. For a latent space of $$J$$ dimensions, it is computed as:

$$
\text{KL} \big( q_\phi(z \mid x) \parallel p(z) \big) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \left( \mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_j^2 \right)
$$

where $$\mu_j$$ and $$\sigma_j$$ are the mean and the standard deviation of the $$j$$-th dimension of $$z$$. As an example, an encoder that outputs $$\mu = 2$$ and $$\sigma = 0.5$$ for a one-dimensional $$z$$ pays a penalty of $$\frac{1}{2}(2^2 + 0.5^2 - 1 - \log 0.5^2) = 2.32$$ [nats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nat_(unit)). The penalty is zero only when $$\mu_j = 0$$ and $$\sigma_j = 1$$, i.e. when the encoder outputs the prior: the term $$\mu_j^2$$ pulls the means toward 0, and the terms $$\sigma_j^2 - \log \sigma_j^2$$ keep the standard deviations close to 1. The reconstruction term pulls the other way, toward precise codes (a small $$\sigma_j$$) that tell the datapoints apart, and the ELBO balances the two.

The ELBO is a lower bound of the log-likelihood, because the log-likelihood of a datapoint is the ELBO plus the KL divergence of the encoder from the true posterior, which is never negative (Eq. 1 in the paper):

$$
\log p_\theta(x) = \text{ELBO}(\theta, \phi; x) + \text{KL} \big( q_\phi(z \mid x) \parallel p_\theta(z \mid x) \big) \ge \text{ELBO}(\theta, \phi; x)
$$

Therefore, maximizing the ELBO with respect to $$\theta$$ and $$\phi$$ does two things at once (section 2.2.1 of the Introduction to Variational Autoencoders): it approximately maximizes the likelihood $$p_\theta(x)$$, so the decoder becomes a better generative model, and it minimizes the KL divergence of the encoder from the true posterior, so the encoder becomes a better approximation of it.

### The reparameterization trick

The loss is minimized with stochastic gradient descent, which needs the gradient of the ELBO with respect to the parameters $$\phi$$ of the encoder. The authors noticed that the sampling induces noise in this gradient (or, put differently, that $$z$$ is randomly generated and cannot be backpropagated): the first term of the ELBO is an expectation over $$q_\phi(z \mid x)$$, a distribution that depends on $$\phi$$ itself. The usual Monte Carlo estimator of such a gradient, the **score function estimator** (also called REINFORCE), is computed as:

$$
\nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)} \left[ f(z) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)} \left[ f(z) \, \nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z \mid x) \right]
$$

where $$f(z) = \log p_\theta(x \mid z)$$ is the objective. It uses only the value of $$f$$, not its gradient, and it has a very high variance, which makes it impractical (section 2.2 of the paper). To counteract that, the authors propose the **[reparameterization trick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reparameterization_trick)**.

The reparameterization trick moves the randomness of the sampling into an auxiliary noise variable, through a deterministic transformation. Instead of sampling $$z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$$, $$z$$ is rewritten as $$z = \mu + \sigma \cdot \epsilon$$, where $$\epsilon$$ follows the standard Gaussian, i.e. $$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$. For a vector $$z$$, the product is element-wise, $$z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$$, with $$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$.

Why does this work?
- The randomness is now isolated in $$\epsilon$$, **which does not depend on the model parameters**.
- The computation of $$z$$ becomes a differentiable function of $$\mu$$ and $$\sigma$$ (the outputs of the encoder), allowing gradients to flow through $$\mu$$ and $$\sigma$$ during backpropagation, because **the transformation $$z = \mu + \sigma \cdot \epsilon$$ is fully differentiable with respect to these parameters**.

As an example, take the encoder output of the KL example above, $$\mu = 2$$ and $$\sigma = 0.5$$. A noise sample $$\epsilon = -1.4$$ gives the code $$z = 2 + 0.5 \cdot (-1.4) = 1.3$$, with the derivatives $$\partial z / \partial \mu = 1$$ and $$\partial z / \partial \sigma = \epsilon = -1.4$$: the gradient of the objective reaches the encoder through $$z$$ by the chain rule, as through any other layer. The estimate is also much less noisy. For the objective $$f(z) = z^2$$, whose gradient with respect to $$\mu$$ is $$2\mu = 4$$, one sample of the reparameterized estimator $$2z$$ has a standard deviation of 1.0, against 11.1 for one sample of the score function estimator.

Putting the pieces together gives the SGVB estimator of the ELBO of a datapoint (Eq. 10 in the paper), with one sample of the noise per datapoint, as in the experiments of the paper:

$$
\text{ELBO}(\theta, \phi; x) \approx \log p_\theta(x \mid z) - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \left( \mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_j^2 \right)
$$

where $$z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$$ is computed from one sample $$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$. In practice, a training step passes each datapoint of a mini-batch through the encoder to get $$\mu$$ and $$\sigma$$, samples $$\epsilon$$ and computes $$z$$. Then, it passes $$z$$ through the decoder to get the reconstruction term, adds the closed-form KL, and backpropagates the negative of the result, the loss, through both networks.

### Generating new data

Once the VAE is trained, the encoder is not needed to generate data: a sample of the prior, $$z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$, passes through the decoder, and the mean of $$p_\theta(x \mid z)$$ (e.g. the intensity of each pixel of an image) is a new datapoint. This works because the KL term kept the codes of the training data close to the prior, so that a sample of the prior falls where the decoder was trained. With a two-dimensional $$z$$, the paper decodes a grid of codes into a map of the digits that the decoder generates across the latent space (Figure 4 of the paper). The encoder remains useful to map a datapoint to its latent representation, e.g. to compare datapoints.

### Results

The paper tested the VAE on the MNIST and Frey Face datasets and compared the variational lower bound and estimated marginal likelihood, demonstrating improved results over the [wake-sleep algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake-sleep_algorithm). The encoder and the decoder had one hidden layer each (500 hidden units for MNIST, 200 for Frey Face). With latent spaces of 2 to 200 dimensions, AEVB converged considerably faster than wake-sleep and reached a better lower bound in all experiments (Figure 2 of the paper).

### Detour: why maximize the expected value?

In the Bayesian setup, it is common for the loss function to be the sum of the expected values of several terms.

It all comes down to the [Law of large numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_large_numbers). According to the law, the average of the outcomes of a large number of trials should be close to the expected value, and it tends to get closer to the expected value as the number of trials increases.

Imagine a game (Blackjack, Slots, Rock-Paper-Scissors) that can be repeated as many times as desired. Because of the law of large numbers, the value of the wins over $$n$$ iterations of the game will approximate $$n \mathbb{E}(X)$$, where $$X$$ is the value won in one iteration. In practice, the goal is to optimize the problem according to the average outcome of the experiment over many repetitions, i.e. to maximize its expected value, which is not necessarily the most likely outcome.

So in practice, the law of large numbers will often guarantee a better outcome over the long run, and that outcome is maximized by maximizing the *expected* outcome of the optimization. In the same way, the expected values in the losses of this post are estimated by averages over the samples of a mini-batch.

## Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)

Credit: most content in this section is a summary of the paper [Generative Adversarial Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661), published at NIPS 2014 (the conference now called NeurIPS), by Ian Goodfellow and colleagues at the University of Montreal.

The paper introduces a new generative model composed of two models trained simultaneously:
- a generative model G that captures the data distribution; and
- a discriminative model D that estimates the probability that a sample came from the training data rather than G;

The training procedure for G is to maximize the probability of D making a mistake. This framework corresponds to a [minimax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimax) two-player game. The name *adversarial* comes from the fact that the generative model faces the discriminative model, which learns whether a sample is from the model distribution or from the data distribution. Quoting the authors:

> "The generative model can be thought of as analogous to a team of counterfeiters, trying to produce fake currency and use it without detection, while the discriminative model is analogous to the police, trying to detect the counterfeit currency. Competition in this game drives both teams to improve their methods until the counterfeits are indistiguishable from the genuine articles."

### Mathematical setup

The goal of the generative model is to generate samples by passing random noise $$z$$ through a [multilayer perceptron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilayer_perceptron).
- The **generator** $$G(z; \theta_g)$$ is a multilayer perceptron with parameters $$\theta_g$$.
- $$p_z(z)$$ is the prior on the input noise variables.
- The generator $$G$$ implicitly defines a probability distribution $$p_g$$ as the distribution of the samples $$G(z)$$ obtained when $$z \sim p_z$$;
- The goal of the generator is to make $$p_g$$ close to the distribution of the data $$p_\text{data}$$, to fake it "well enough" and trick the discriminator;

The discriminative model is also a multilayer perceptron.
- The **discriminator** $$D(x; \theta_d)$$ takes the input $$x$$ and, based on its DNN parameters $$\theta_d$$, outputs a single scalar representing the probability that $$x$$ came from the data rather than $$p_g$$;

### The minimax loss

The discriminator $$D$$ is trained to best guess the source of the data, i.e. to maximize the probability of assigning the correct label to both the training examples and the samples from $$G$$. 

The generator $$G$$ is simultaneously trained to minimize $$\log(1-D(G(z)))$$, i.e. to be able to fake the data well enough. In practice, $$D$$ and $$G$$ are playing a two-player minimax game with value function $$V(D, G)$$:

$$
\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x} \sim p_{\text{data}}(\boldsymbol{x})} [\log D(\boldsymbol{x})] + \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z} \sim p_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{z})} [\log (1 - D(G(\boldsymbol{z})))].
$$

The loss function is a sum of two penalization terms:
- in the first term, the discriminator is optimized such that, in the long run, all real inputs (drawn from the data, i.e. $$x \sim p_\text{data}$$) are expected to be classified as correctly as possible;
- in the second term, the discriminator and the generator are optimized with opposite goals on all fake inputs (generated by the generator from $$z \sim p_z$$): the discriminator maximizes this term, to classify fake inputs as fake, and the generator minimizes it, so that the discriminator classifies fake inputs as real;

Finally, notice there is a $$\log$$ term added to both expected value terms. This is because the objective function is the product of multiple probabilities (of $$D$$ assigning the correct label to each input), in this case $$D(x) * (1-D(G(z)))$$. Adding the $$\log$$ makes the optimization return the same optima (as $$\log$$ is monotonic), while making it faster and more numerically stable. The expectation term is also added to the loss because the goal is to minimize the loss that is expected after several samples (law of large numbers). Thus:

$$
\mathbb{E} \left[ \log(D(x) * (1-D(G(z)))) \right] = \mathbb{E} \left[ \log(D(x)) \right] + \mathbb{E} \left[ \log(1-D(G(z))) \right]
$$

In other words, $$V(D, G)$$ is the log-likelihood of the correct labels of the inputs of $$D$$, 1 for real inputs and 0 for generated ones. The binary cross-entropy of a prediction $$\hat{y}$$ for a label $$y$$ is $$-y \log \hat{y} - (1 - y) \log (1 - \hat{y})$$, i.e. $$-\log D(x)$$ for a real input and $$-\log(1 - D(G(z)))$$ for a generated one. So $$V$$ is the negative of the binary cross-entropy of $$D$$, with one expectation over the real inputs and one over the generated inputs. This cross-entropy is the standard loss of a binary classifier with a sigmoid output (section 3.2.1 of the NIPS 2016 tutorial on GANs): the discriminator maximizes $$V$$ by minimizing its classification loss, and the generator minimizes $$V$$ by maximizing it.

In section 4 (theoretical results), the authors show that, given enough capacity for the discriminator and the generator, the minimax game recovers the data-generating distribution. For a fixed generator, the optimal discriminator is given by (Proposition 1 in the paper):

$$
D^*_G(x) = \frac{p_\text{data}(x)}{p_\text{data}(x) + p_g(x)}
$$

i.e. the probability that a sample at $$x$$ comes from the data rather than from the generator, when both are drawn equally often. As an example, at a point where $$p_\text{data}(x) = 3 \, p_g(x)$$, the optimal discriminator outputs $$3 / (3 + 1) = 0.75$$, and where $$p_g(x) = p_\text{data}(x)$$, it outputs $$1/2$$: it cannot tell the two apart.

With the optimal discriminator, the value function becomes the criterion that the generator minimizes, $$C(G) = \max_D V(D, G)$$, which is computed as (in the proof of Theorem 1 of the paper):

$$
C(G) = -\log 4 + 2 \cdot \text{JSD}(p_\text{data} \parallel p_g)
$$

where JSD is the [Jensen-Shannon divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jensen%E2%80%93Shannon_divergence), the mean of the KL divergences of $$p_\text{data}$$ and $$p_g$$ from their average $$(p_\text{data} + p_g) / 2$$. Unlike the KL divergence, it is symmetric, and it ranges from 0, when the two distributions are equal, to $$\log 2$$, when they do not overlap. Therefore, this minimax game has a global optimum, $$C(G) = -\log 4$$, reached only when $$p_g = p_\text{data}$$.

However, the proof that the training algorithm converges to this optimum (Proposition 2 in the paper) assumes that $$D$$ reaches its optimum given $$G$$ at every step, and that $$p_g$$ itself is updated. In practice, the algorithm updates the parameters $$\theta_g$$ of a multilayer perceptron, which introduces multiple critical points in the parameter space, and the authors use it despite its lack of theoretical guarantees.

### Training algorithm

The training algorithm is the following:

$$
\begin{array}{l}
\hline
\textbf{Algorithm 1} \text{ Minibatch stochastic gradient descent training of generative} \\
\text{adversarial nets. The number of steps to apply to the discriminator, } k \text{, is a} \\
\text{hyperparameter. We used } k = 1 \text{, the least expensive option, in our experiments.} \\
\hline
\quad \textbf{for} \text{ number of training iterations } \textbf{do} \\
\qquad \textbf{for}\ k \text{ steps } \textbf{do} \\
\qquad\quad \bullet \text{ Sample minibatch of } m \text{ noise samples } \{\boldsymbol{z}^{(1)}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{z}^{(m)}\} \text{ from noise prior } p_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{z}). \\
\qquad\quad \bullet \text{ Sample minibatch of } m \text{ examples } \{\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{x}^{(m)}\} \text{ from data generating distribution} \\
\qquad\quad p_{\text{data}}(\boldsymbol{x}). \\
\qquad\quad \bullet \text{ Update the discriminator by ascending its stochastic gradient:} \\
\hspace{9em} \displaystyle \nabla_{\theta_d} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \left[ \log D\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\right) + \log\left(1 - D\left(G\left(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\right)\right)\right) \right]. \\
\qquad \textbf{end for} \\
\qquad \bullet \text{ Sample minibatch of } m \text{ noise samples } \{\boldsymbol{z}^{(1)}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{z}^{(m)}\} \text{ from noise prior } p_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{z}). \\
\qquad \bullet \text{ Update the generator by descending its stochastic gradient:} \\
\hspace{12.8em} \displaystyle \nabla_{\theta_g} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \log\left(1 - D\left(G\left(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\right)\right)\right). \\
\quad \textbf{end for} \\
\text{The gradient-based updates can use any standard gradient-based learning rule.} \\
\text{We used momentum in our experiments.} \\
\hline
\end{array}
$$

### Challenge: different convergence speeds of the generator and the discriminator

Optimizing $$D$$ to completion before each step of $$G$$ is "computationally prohibitive, and on finite datasets would result in overfitting". Instead, training alternates between $$k$$ steps of optimizing $$D$$ and one step of optimizing $$G$$ (the authors use $$k=1$$). This keeps $$D$$ near its optimal solution, as long as $$G$$ changes slowly enough.

### Challenge: saturation of the generator loss

The loss equation may not provide sufficient gradient for $$G$$ to learn well. Early in learning, when $$G$$ is poor, $$D$$ can reject samples with high confidence because they are clearly different from the training data. In this case, $$\log(1 − D(G(z)))$$ saturates.

Saturation means that $$D(G(z))$$ is close to 0, where this term is flat. The output of $$D$$ is the [sigmoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmoid_function) of a [logit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logit) $$a$$, $$D = \operatorname{sigmoid}(a)$$, and the derivative of $$\log(1 - \operatorname{sigmoid}(a))$$ with respect to $$a$$ is $$-\operatorname{sigmoid}(a)$$, close to 0: $$-0.01$$ for $$D(G(z)) = 0.01$$. In practice, this means that this term of the loss function will not provide any signal (gradients) for the update of the generator, making it useless: when the discriminator performs significantly better than the generator, the updates to the generator are either inaccurate or disappear.

This leads to two main problems:
- mathematically, the update of the parameters is zero or very small, so progress through the loss landscape is very slow;
- computationally, arithmetic operations over very small values lead to incorrect (or "always zero") results due to insufficient floating-point precision in the processor (typically 16 or 32 bits); 

The workaround suggested by the authors is to change the algorithm for training the generator: instead of minimizing $$\log(1 - D(G(z)))$$, they maximize $$\log D(G(z))$$, known as the **non-saturating** loss of the generator. This keeps the same fixed point of the dynamics of $$G$$ and $$D$$, while the optimization landscape "provides much stronger gradients early in learning": the derivative of $$\log \operatorname{sigmoid}(a)$$ with respect to $$a$$ is $$1 - \operatorname{sigmoid}(a)$$, close to 1, i.e. 0.99 for $$D(G(z)) = 0.01$$, 99 times larger in magnitude than the gradient of the original loss. The pseudocode above shows the original update, which minimizes $$\log(1 - D(G(z)))$$.

### Challenge: mode collapse

The original publication mentions this challenge only briefly, as "the Helvetica scenario" in which $$G$$ collapses too many values of $$z$$ to the same value of $$x$$. In a regular scenario, GANs are expected to generate a range of outputs, or ideally, a new random valid output for every random input to the generator. Instead, it may happen that **sometimes the output is monotonous, i.e. a range of very similar outputs**.

This phenomenon is called **[mode collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_collapse)** and is [detailed in this paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.09673) as: "This undesirable event occurs when the model can only fit a few modes of the data distribution, while ignoring the majority of them". In this article, the authors propose a workaround using second-order gradient information. However, this is a field of intense research and several solutions have been proposed, to name a few: a different loss function ([Wasserstein loss](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.07875)), [Unrolled GANs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.02163.pdf), [Conditional GANs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.1784), [VQ-VAEs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00937), etc. Unrolled GANs incorporate the current and future discriminator's classifications in the loss function, so that the generator cannot over-optimize for a single discriminator.

## Comparison of VAEs and GANs

Both models generate a new datapoint with one pass of a sample of the prior through a network, the decoder or the generator, but they learn this network in different ways. The VAE minimizes one loss, the negative ELBO, with stochastic gradient descent, and also learns an encoder that maps each datapoint to its latent representation. The GAN has no encoder and no explicit model of the data ("there is no explicit representation of $$p_g(x)$$", section 6 of the paper): its generator learns only from the gradients of the discriminator, in a minimax game whose two players must stay balanced. In terms of compute, a training step of the VAE runs the encoder and the decoder once on a mini-batch. An iteration of Algorithm 1 with $$k = 1$$ runs the generator twice and the discriminator three times: the update of the discriminator runs it on real and on generated data, and the update of the generator on generated data again.

For the sake of comparison, a VAE is easier to train and less prone to mode collapse, as it maximizes a lower bound of the likelihood of every datapoint, while a GAN generates sharper samples. In the words of the Introduction to Variational Autoencoders (section 1.1), VAEs "generate more dispersed samples, but are better density models in terms of the likelihood criterion", while GANs "tend to lack full support over the data". A VAE is the better choice when a latent representation of the data or a bound on its likelihood is needed, which the original GAN does not provide, and a GAN when sample quality matters most.

The [NIPS 2016 tutorial on GANs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.00160) by Ian Goodfellow compares GANs with other generative models and presents the research frontiers of GANs.
